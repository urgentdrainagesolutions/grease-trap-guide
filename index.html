Grease Traps for Commercial Kitchens: A UK Owner's Guide

Fat, oil and grease are the number one cause of blocked drains in restaurants and takeaways. A properly sized and maintained grease trap protects your drains, your neighbours and your licence to trade.

Quick answer: A grease trap (or grease interceptor) sits between your kitchen sinks and the drain. It slows waste water down so fats, oils and grease (FOG) float and separate before they reach the sewer. Food businesses in the UK are expected to stop FOG entering drains, and a grease trap is the most common way to do it.

Why fat, oil and grease are such a problem

FOG is liquid when hot but sets hard as it cools inside pipes. It traps food waste and wet wipes and builds into thick blockages. In sewers these grow into the fatbergs that water companies spend millions removing. Read how this happens in our guide to fat, oil and grease in drains.

Do you legally need a grease trap?

In England it is an offence to put matter into a public sewer that interferes with its free flow, and water companies can recover the cost of clearing blockages from the business that caused them. Many local authorities and water companies expect commercial kitchens to have FOG controls, such as a grease trap, and building regulations require adequate grease separation for new commercial kitchen drainage.

Maintenance checklist

  • Scrape plates and pans into food waste before washing
  • Never pour cooking oil down sinks; collect it for licensed recycling
  • Empty or service the trap on a fixed schedule and keep records
  • Book drain jetting periodically to clear any build-up downstream
  • Train new staff on day one

Signs your grease trap or drains need attention

  • Slow-draining sinks and gurgling
  • Bad smells in the kitchen or outside gullies
  • Grease visible in outside inspection chambers
